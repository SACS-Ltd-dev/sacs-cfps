1. What Cybersecurity Actually Is

If I asked ten people in this room what cybersecurity means, I'd probably get ten different answers. Stopping hackers. Protecting passwords. Firewalls and antivirus. None of those are wrong exactly, but they're all symptoms rather than the definition itself. Here's the version I want you to hold onto:

The working definition I use

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information and systems against unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction.

Notice what I didn't say. I didn't say "stopping hackers," because in my experience a huge share of real security incidents involve no hacker at all. A misconfigured cloud storage bucket. A backup nobody ever tested. An employee who clicked a link on a busy Monday morning. Cybersecurity, at its core, is about managing the gap between what an organization needs to keep working and private, and everything that could realistically interfere with that.

Our running example for this session

Zenith Trust Microfinance Bank

I'm going to use a fictional Lagos based microfinance bank, Zenith Trust Microfinance Bank (ZTMB), throughout this module. ZTMB has three branches in Victoria Island, Ikeja, and Lekki, a mobile banking app, a USSD banking channel for customers without smartphones, around 80 staff, and a core banking application that processes loan disbursements and savings transactions. I chose this kind of organization deliberately: mid-sized, digitally active, and not yet mature in its security posture, because it's close to the kind of organization many of you will work with early in your careers.

The three properties in that definition (confidentiality, integrity, and availability) are usually grouped together as the CIA triad. You've probably met this before at a purely definitional level. Here's what I want it to mean to you operationally, using ZTMB:

Only authorized people or systems can see the information. At ZTMB, a loan officer should see the loan applications assigned to them, but not the salary records of the CEO, and certainly not a full export of the customer database. A confidentiality failure looks like a support staff member browsing customer BVN and account numbers out of curiosity, or a misconfigured cloud bucket exposing loan applications to the open internet.

Information is accurate and hasn't been improperly changed. At ZTMB, a customer's account balance has to reflect their actual transaction history, not something alterable by bypassing the core banking application. An integrity failure looks like an attacker, or a corrupt insider, editing a loan record to approve a disbursement that was never authorized.

Systems and information are accessible to authorized users when they're needed. At ZTMB, if the core banking application goes down during business hours, customers can't withdraw savings and branches can't process loans. An availability failure looks like ransomware encrypting the core banking database, or something as unglamorous as a generator failing during a power outage with no backup power plan in place.

Every security decision you'll make in this programme, every control, every investigation, every recommendation, traces back to protecting one or more of these three properties for a specific asset. Keep that sentence close.

2. Assets and Attack Surface

An asset is anything of value to the organization that needs protecting: data, systems, applications, people, physical infrastructure, even reputation. Not all assets matter equally, and figuring out which ones matter most is usually a security professional's first real job, because you cannot protect everything with the same intensity on a limited budget with limited staff. That describes almost every organization you'll work in, and it's especially true across Nigeria and the wider African security market, where budgets are often tight.

The attack surface is every point where an unauthorized party could try to interact with those assets: every login page, every open network port, every employee who can be phished, every third party integration.

Look closely at the diagram. Assets aren't only servers. Customers using the mobile app are part of the picture too, since their devices and behavior are part of the attack surface. So is a category I haven't defined for you yet: an unknown actor probing the web gateway. That's a threat, and it's the next idea I want to build with you.

3. Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Risk

These three words get used interchangeably in casual conversation, and that habit is exactly what I want to break in this session. They're distinct, and mixing them up leads to bad decisions: spending money on the wrong control, or reporting the wrong thing to your manager.

TermWhat it meansZTMB example
Threat Anything that could cause harm: a person, a group, or an event with the potential to exploit a vulnerability. A criminal group running phishing campaigns against Nigerian bank staff to steal login credentials.
Vulnerability A weakness that could be exploited. The legacy loan application hasn't received a security patch in fourteen months (see Figure 1).
Risk The likelihood of a threat exploiting a vulnerability, combined with the impact if it happens. Given the phishing threat and the unpatched loan app, I'd call this a high risk of unauthorized loan disbursement fraud within the next twelve months.

A mistake I see constantly from beginners

Saying "our biggest risk is hackers" confuses threat and risk, and it isn't useful to a manager. A far more useful sentence: "Our biggest risk is unauthorized fund transfer through the unpatched loan application, because it's reachable from the internet and we know phishing campaigns targeting Nigerian financial staff are active right now." That sentence names the vulnerability, the threat, and implies the impact. That's the professional version, and it's the standard I'll hold you to.

A simple way to think about risk

We'll refine this considerably in Module 16, but for now this rough formula will serve you well:

Risk is roughly Likelihood (of the threat exploiting the vulnerability) multiplied by Impact (if it succeeds)

That's why a low likelihood, high impact event, such as a core banking database breach, and a high likelihood, low impact event, such as an employee's personal email being phished with no link to work systems, can both deserve your attention, just very differently.

4. Security Controls

A control is any measure that reduces risk, whether by reducing the likelihood of a threat succeeding, reducing the impact if it does, or both. I usually classify controls by when they act relative to an incident:

Preventive controls. Stop it before it happens

These stop an incident from occurring in the first place. Multi-factor authentication on ZTMB's internet banking, firewall rules blocking unnecessary inbound ports, security awareness training so staff don't fall for phishing at all, locked server room doors.

Detective controls. Notice it while it happens, or after

These identify that something has gone wrong. A SIEM alert on unusual after hours logins to the core banking server, CCTV covering the server room, a bank reconciliation process that flags unexpected transactions, an intrusion detection system watching branch network traffic.

Corrective controls. Fix it and reduce the chance it happens again

These limit damage and restore normal operation once an incident is detected. Restoring the core banking database from a clean backup, revoking a compromised staff account, patching the exploited vulnerability, updating the incident response playbook so the same gap doesn't reappear.

5. Defense in Depth and Security by Design

Defense in depth means never relying on a single control to protect a critical asset. If ZTMB relied solely on a firewall to protect the core banking database, one firewall misconfiguration exposes everything behind it. Instead, layer your controls: network segmentation, a web application firewall, strong authentication, database access logging, encryption at rest, so that one failure doesn't equal total compromise.

Security by design means building security into a system from the start rather than bolting it on afterward. A mobile banking app designed with security by design encrypts data in transit by default and validates every input on the server side from day one, rather than shipping a version 1.0 without these protections and adding them only after a breach forces the issue.

6. The Security Lifecycle

Security isn't a one-time project. It's a continuous cycle. Here's a simplified version that I want you to hold onto; we'll expand it considerably later:

  1. Identify. Know your assets, threats, and vulnerabilities.
  2. Protect. Implement controls.
  3. Detect. Monitor for signs that controls have failed or a threat is active.
  4. Respond. Act on what you detect.
  5. Recover. Restore normal operations and improve based on what you learned.

This exact structure reappears in much greater depth in Module 11, on incident response and digital forensics. For now, just notice that it's a loop, not a straight line. Recover feeds back into Identify. Every incident should update what you understand about your own assets and vulnerabilities.

7. Technical, Administrative, and Physical Security

Controls aren't only technical. A complete security programme addresses three dimensions:

DimensionWhat it coversZTMB example
TechnicalTechnology based controlsFirewalls, encryption, MFA, endpoint monitoring
AdministrativePolicies, procedures, training, governanceAn acceptable use policy, background checks for tellers, an incident response plan, onboarding and offboarding checklists
PhysicalProtecting physical access to facilities and equipmentLocked server racks, visitor sign-in at branches, CCTV, secured cash points

A mistake I see often from beginners is treating cybersecurity as purely technical. In practice, plenty of real incidents, including several well documented cases across Nigerian and African financial institutions, trace back to administrative failures (no offboarding process, so a departed staff member's access was never revoked) or physical failures (an unattended workstation left logged in at a branch), not exotic technical exploits.

8. Cybersecurity Career Domains

Cybersecurity isn't one job. It's a profession made up of distinct disciplines, each demanding a different mix of skills and temperament. You'll get direct, hands-on exposure to most of these across this programme, deliberately, so that your eventual specialization choice in Module 18 is based on real experience rather than guesswork or hype.

Offensive security professionals think and act like an attacker, under strict authorization, to find weaknesses before real attackers do. You'll get your first real taste of this reasoning in Module 6.

Defensive security professionals design and maintain the controls that protect systems: architecture, hardening, network segmentation. I'll take you deeper into this in Module 8.

SOC analysts monitor systems continuously, triage alerts, and decide what's worth escalating. That's Module 9.

Digital forensics and incident response professionals investigate after something has gone wrong, reconstructing what happened from evidence. That's Module 11.

Governance, risk, and compliance professionals connect technical security to business risk, policy, and regulatory obligations, including Nigeria's NDPA. That's Module 16.

Keep an open mind through this session. You aren't choosing a path yet. I just want you noticing your own reactions as we go.

Session Summary

Cybersecurity protects the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of assets against threats exploiting vulnerabilities, using preventive, detective, and corrective controls, layered through defense in depth, across technical, administrative, and physical dimensions, in a continuous lifecycle. It's also a multidisciplinary profession, not a single job, and I want you to remember that as we go deeper.
